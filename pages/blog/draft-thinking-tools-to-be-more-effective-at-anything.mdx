Hey you! You probably want to get stuff done.[^whatdo]

Many people describe me as a person who gets things done! A good chunk of this (I think) is having a toolbox of thinking tools,[^routinedefinition] and deploying them at the right times.

In this post I'll list my toolbox of thinking tools.[^novel] Each idea is written independently, so you don't need to read it in order.

If you want, you can imagine these as "[Claude skills](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/12512176-what-are-skills), but for humans".[^clauderelevance] Hopefully helpful while humans are still driving the thinking.[^agisoon]

(Also because this blog is designed to be easily readable by agents, you can also use them as "Claude skills, but for humans, for Claude". That is, get Claude to notice when a routine might be relevant and help you to apply them![^aiprompt])

## Choosing what to work on

### Backwards chaining

When trying to brainstorm options to achieve a goal: identify that goal and work backwards from it.

[Read the full backchaining article.](../backchaining/)

### Create surface area for luck

A lot of success comes from randomness - but you can build your own luck: do more, work in public, and prepare to seize windows of opportunity.

[Read the full article on creating surface area for luck.](../create-surface-area-for-luck/)

### Optimize only the constraint

When improving a system with many parts: find the bottleneck and improve only that.

[Read the full article on optimizing the constraint.](../optimize-only-the-constraint/)

### Framestorming

Brainstorm different framings of the problem, not just solutions to it.

[Read the full framestorming article.](../framestorming/)

### Musk's 5 step design process

Eliminate work before optimising it: make requirements less dumb, delete everything you can, simplify, accelerate cycle time, and only then automate.

[Read the full article on Musk's 5 step design process.](../musks-5-step-design-process/)

### Accept redundancy

Redundant work isn't always bad: coordination is often more expensive than duplication, and redundant bets increase the chance of success on important, uncertain outcomes.

[Read the full article on accepting redundancy.](../accept-redundancy/)

### Hunt for 100x opportunities

Most markets you can care about are wildly inefficient, so the best option is often 100x better than the average one. Spend real time generating ambitious options and selecting between them.

[Read the full article on hunting for 100x opportunities.](../hunt-for-100x-opportunities/)

### Pick up the trillion-dollar bill

Great opportunities do sit unclaimed, especially in neglected areas. Ask "why hasn't anyone done this?" - and when there's no good answer, pursue it.

[Read the full article on picking up the trillion-dollar bill.](../pick-up-the-trillion-dollar-bill/)

### Find problems, not applications for solutions

Start from an important problem and test many solutions against it, rather than taking your solution and hunting for problems it fits.

[Read the full article on finding problems, not applications for solutions.](../find-problems-not-applications-for-solutions/)

### Use leverage against weakness

Concentrate your relative strength against the problem or opponent's relative weakness - rather than grinding away at eliminating your own weaknesses.

[Read the full article on using leverage against weakness.](../use-leverage-against-weakness/)

### Be pareto-best in the world

Being the best in the world at one thing is hard; being the best at an intersection of a few skills is surprisingly achievable - and nearly as valuable.

[Read the full article on being pareto-best in the world.](../be-pareto-best-in-the-world/)

### Ask if the answer changes the decision

Before researching something, check whether the decision actually changes depending on the answer. If not, skip it.

[Read the full article on asking if the answer changes the decision.](../ask-if-the-answer-changes-the-decision/)

### Defend slack

Don't run at maximum capacity. Slack is what lets you absorb disruption, seize opportunities, and keep functioning - and queueing theory says the last few percent of "efficiency" are the most expensive.

[Read the full article on defending slack.](../defend-slack/)

### Be a new homunculus

Guilt, sunk costs and old commitments keep people working on things too long. Pretend you're a new lil guy at the levers, with no reason to honour any of it.

[Read the full article on being a new homunculus.](../be-a-new-homunculus/)

## Figuring out what's true

### Adopt a scout mindset

Always, but especially when a belief is comfortable: actually try to figure out the truth.

[Julia Galef's frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Scout_Mindset): the soldier's job is to defend a position; the scout's job is to map the terrain accurately, because their side will live or die by that map. Most reasoning, most of the time, is soldier work - defending, performing, pattern-matching. Actually trying is rarer and more powerful than it sounds.

A sharp test of the difference: a pundit predicting on TV versus someone with money on the line in a prediction market. Only one of them is actually trying to be right.

#### Related concepts
- [Interrogate the claim](#interrogate-the-claim): scout mindset applied to a specific claim
- [Hold concrete opinions, update honestly](#hold-concrete-opinions-update-honestly): scout mindset applied to your own public positions
- [Find the crux](#find-the-crux): scout mindset applied to a disagreement

### Interrogate the claim

When a claim sounds damning or too clean: dig in, then dig in again.

"The AI model only approves male applicants" - dig in, and it turns out only men applied. Okay, with equal applications it approves more men - dig in, and the male applicants were more qualified. And so on: each layer can flip the conclusion.

#### Related concepts
- [Adopt a scout mindset](#adopt-a-scout-mindset): you have to actually want the claim to be tested, not just confirmed
- [Suspect mess or misalignment, not evil](#suspect-mess-or-misalignment-not-evil): a good prior for what you'll find at the bottom of the digging

### Suspect mess or misalignment, not evil

When a heavily scrutinised public decision looks obviously bad: work out which of two boring explanations applies before reaching for malice.

Usually either (1) it's much messier and more complex up close, or (2) the incentives point the wrong way - in which case it shouldn't surprise you, and complaining about it won't fix it. Fix the incentive or accept the outcome.

### Making decisions with made-up statistics

When facing a decision "too uncertain to quantify": make up numbers anyway.

[If it's worth doing, it's worth doing with made-up statistics](https://slatestarcodex.com/2013/05/02/if-its-worth-doing-its-worth-doing-with-made-up-statistics/). A rough estimate with stated assumptions exposes your reasoning to criticism in a way "it depends" never does. Thinking in explicit probabilities, or at least scope-sensitively, is the same move applied to beliefs.

This is the skill behind the classic [Fermi question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_problem): how many piano tuners are there in Chicago? Population, households per piano, tunings per year, tunings a tuner can do - four made-up numbers multiply out to a decent answer. And because each number is stated, each can be argued with and improved. "It depends" can't be.

#### Related concepts
- [Ask if the answer changes the decision](../ask-if-the-answer-changes-the-decision/) first - the fastest estimate is the one you don't need
- [Hunt for 100x opportunities](../hunt-for-100x-opportunities/): rough numbers are enough to compare options that differ by orders of magnitude
- [Run the experiment](#run-the-experiment): when even made-up numbers feel too shaky, reality is available

### Find the crux

When a disagreement is going in circles: find the belief that would change your conclusion if you changed your mind about it.

Ideally find a [double crux](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/exa5kmvopeRyfJgCy/double-crux-a-strategy-for-mutual-understanding) - one that plays that role for both of you.

!["We should rewrite it" and "We should patch it" clash at the top; their surface arguments sit below; both trace down to one shared highlighted box: the crux, "v2 must ship within 6 months" - if this flipped, we'd both switch sides](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/double-crux.svg)

#### Related concepts
- [Ask if the answer changes the decision](../ask-if-the-answer-changes-the-decision/): a crux is precisely a question whose answer changes the decision
- [Refuse the idiot plot](#refuse-the-idiot-plot): saying the awkward thing out loud is often how the crux surfaces

### Run the experiment

When a debate could be settled by trying it: try it.

The cheap experiment is usually faster than the analysis that avoids it. And when theory and measurement disagree, trust the measurement - and feel appropriately bad about your theory.

In [Tom Wujec's spaghetti-tower challenge](https://www.tomwujec.com/marshmallowchallenge) (build the tallest structure from dry spaghetti, tape and string, marshmallow on top), kindergartners reliably beat business school graduates. The kids start sticking spaghetti together immediately - build, wobble, fix, rebuild - while the adults plan and jockey, execute a single design, and discover in the final seconds that marshmallows are heavier than they look.

#### Related concepts
- [Small cycles, small batches](#small-cycles-small-batches) is this routine as a way of working, not a one-off
- [Making decisions with made-up statistics](#making-decisions-with-made-up-statistics): for when the experiment genuinely is expensive

### Own it all the way down

When you own something that's blocked: know who has it, why they haven't actioned it, whether they know the priority, what would help them, and what the alternative routes are - basically all the way down.

"It's been stuck with legal for two weeks, no idea what's taking them so long" is not ownership.

#### Related concepts
- [Find another way](#find-another-way): knowing the blocker in detail is what makes the alternative routes visible
- [Interrogate the claim](#interrogate-the-claim): the same "keep asking why" muscle, pointed at your own project

### Become a narrow expert

When a problem lands in someone else's professional domain: get AI to do the first hundred hours of reading, and become a narrow expert on your specific question.

Lawyers, doctors and accountants have real knowledge and experience, but they're not doing magic - with diligence this works surprisingly fast. Rare-disease patients do this out of necessity: they often know the literature on their condition better than a generalist doctor, because they've read all of it and the doctor read some of it years ago. Still defer to experts, but feel free to ask why and understand it (and ask AI first so you're not burning their time on basics).

Related: whatever you're doing, someone has probably done it well before - find them and interview them.

I've done this myself: I ended up reading enough of the law on lasting power of attorney to write [a guide on how the forms should work](../poa-better/).

#### Related concepts
- [Be AI-legible](#be-ai-legible): the infrastructure version of "AI does the first hundred hours"
- [Interrogate the claim](#interrogate-the-claim): expertise is mostly knowing which "why?"s to ask next

### Be AI-legible

Ongoing habit: gather your context in a way that's digestible by AI systems.

Auto-transcribe meetings, keep notes somewhere MCP-accessible, write things down in one queryable place ([here's my setup](https://www.pulsemcp.com/posts/effective-patterns-for-advanced-mcp-usage)). The returns are large and compounding, and this is the one entry on this list that couldn't have existed five years ago.

#### Related concepts
- [Sharpen the axe](#sharpen-the-axe): AI-legibility is axe-sharpening for the AI era
- [Become a narrow expert](#become-a-narrow-expert): works much better when the AI can see your actual context

## Executing

### Build feedback loops

When you're going to do something more than once: spend some of your effort building the loop that tells you how you're doing, not just doing the thing.

Most work has no natural feedback loop. You write, ship, advise or decide - and nothing comes back, or what comes back is so delayed and noisy that you can't learn from it. So you repeat the same mistakes confidently for years.

![Two panels. Left, 'no loop: guessing' - do a lot of work, ship it eventually, find out it was wrong months later: one slow, expensive lesson. Right, 'a loop: hillclimbing' - a tight cycle of try something, see the result, adjust: many cheap lessons, compounding](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/feedback-loops.svg)

A good loop is fast, honest and cheap:
- **Fast**, because a loop that closes in a day teaches you far more per month than one that closes in a quarter.
- **Honest**, because the point is to find out you're wrong. Metrics you can game, or friends who only say nice things, give you a loop that feels good and teaches nothing.
- **Cheap**, because you'll only keep running it if it's easy. A five-minute check you actually do beats a perfect review process you skip.

Concretely: write down predictions before you find out, so you can tell whether you were right. Ask people who'll actually tell you the bad news. Instrument the thing you care about, even roughly. And when you notice a decision you'll make repeatedly, build the loop before you make it a hundred times.

#### Related concepts
- [Small cycles, small batches](#small-cycles-small-batches): the same idea applied to shipping - each batch is a turn of the loop
- [Run the experiment](#run-the-experiment): an experiment is a feedback loop you build deliberately
- [Recursively self-improve](#recursively-self-improve): retros are a feedback loop pointed at your own process
- [Hold concrete opinions, update honestly](#hold-concrete-opinions-update-honestly): stating views concretely is what makes feedback possible

### Small cycles, small batches

When building anything for someone else: get something into the hands of real users as soon as possible and figure out why and how it's wrong.

You can't reason your way to what contact will teach you. Ship in small batches, scaling batch size exponentially as confidence grows.

![Three ship-and-learn loops growing exponentially in size: v0.1 with 2 users, v1 with 20 users, v10 with everyone. Caption: each loop is ship, learn why it's wrong, grow the batch](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/small-cycles.svg)

There's a well-known parable about this from [*Art & Fear*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_%26_Fear): a pottery teacher grades half the class on the quantity of pots produced, and half on producing a single perfect pot. The best pots all come from the quantity group - they spent the term making and learning from mistakes, while the quality group theorised about perfection. (The story is a lightly-fictionalised retelling, but the mechanism is real either way.)

#### Related concepts
- [Accelerate cycle time](../musks-5-step-design-process/#step-4-accelerate-cycle-time) is Musk's version of the same step
- [Run the experiment](#run-the-experiment): each shipped batch is an experiment about what users actually need
- [Half-ass it with everything you've got](#half-ass-it-with-everything-youve-got): small early batches justify a deliberately lower quality bar

### Diverge, then converge

When you notice yourself evaluating ideas mid-brainstorm: stop - brainstorm then prune, don't do both at once.

While diverging, deliberately include dumb ideas and "reward hacks" against the stated goal: they often spur the good ideas, and occasionally _are_ the answer. Inversion helps too: describe the really obvious way to fail, then do the opposite.

![A diamond shape: the left half expands ('diverge: generate, don't judge', with idea dots including an amber 'dumb' one), the right half narrows ('converge: now prune hard') to a single pick](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/double-diamond.svg)

![Two trees: the first branches freely to six leaves; the second has the same branches pruned to gray dashes except one bold surviving path. Caption: doing both at once does both badly](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/diverge-tree.svg)

#### Related concepts
- [Framestorming](../framestorming/): diverge on the problem framing, not just on solutions
- [Backchaining](../backchaining/) and forward chaining are both divergence techniques - use each deliberately

### PIOSEE

When a situation is chaotic and you don't know where to start: Problem, Information, Options, Select, Execute, Evaluate - a decision loop from aviation.

The value isn't these specific letters (aviation uses several variants); it's having _any_ explicit loop, because under pressure people skip straight from problem to execution. [US Airways flight 1549](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Airways_Flight_1549) is the famous demonstration: about three and a half minutes from bird strike to water, and the cockpit transcript is a decision loop run under maximum pressure - establish the problem (both engines out), gather information, weigh the options (LaGuardia? Teterboro? the Hudson?), select, execute. All 155 people survived.

#### Related concepts
- [Diverge, then converge](#diverge-then-converge) is the Options-then-Select step slowed down for when you have more than three minutes
- [Do the obvious things](#do-the-obvious-things): most PIOSEE passes conclude with obvious actions - the loop's job is making sure you actually do them

### Half-ass it with everything you've got

Before starting a task: decide what the goal actually requires, then hit that bar efficiently - quality is not the goal.

Sometimes you need to max quality; often good enough today beats excellent next month. ([Nate Soares](https://mindingourway.com/half-assing-it-with-everything-youve-got/).)

*Boundary with quality-before-scale:* calibrate quality low while exploring; once you're scaling, defects scale with you.

#### Related concepts
- [Optimize only the constraint](../optimize-only-the-constraint/): quality on a non-bottleneck is effort the system's output can't even see
- [Small cycles, small batches](#small-cycles-small-batches): early small batches are exactly where the low bar belongs

### Choose simple over easy

When choosing between approaches: choose the simple one, not the easy one.

Easy means low effort now; simple means few entangled concepts. They often conflict - the easy option (another dependency, another special case) frequently makes the system more complex.

The [left-pad incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Npm_left-pad_incident) is the monument to this: thousands of projects took the easy option of a dependency for 11 lines of string-padding code, and when its author unpublished it, build pipelines across the JavaScript ecosystem fell over. Each choice was easy; the resulting system was anything but simple.

(Rich Hickey, [Simple Made Easy](https://www.thestrangeloop.com/2011/simple-made-easy.html).)

#### Related concepts
- [Simplify or optimise the design](../musks-5-step-design-process/#step-3-simplify-or-optimise-the-design) - Musk's step three, after questioning and deleting
- [Use the time-money exchange rate](#use-the-time-money-exchange-rate): easiness is a loan; check the interest rate

### More dakka

When something works a bit: consider just doing a lot more of it before inventing something new.

More attempts and more practice often just make you better. [Zvi's motivating example](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2017/12/02/more-dakka/): light therapy lamps help with seasonal depression, but only a bit - and apparently almost nobody had asked "what if we used _more_ lamps?" An entire wall of them. It worked. The dose everyone uses is often just the dose someone first tried, not the dose that works.

#### Related concepts
- [Overcorrect, then settle](#overcorrect-then-settle): the same "turn the dial much further" move, applied to behaviour change
- [Small cycles, small batches](#small-cycles-small-batches): more reps is also how the cycles compound

### Overcorrect, then settle

When acting on feedback to change a behaviour: overcorrect, then find the middle.

It beats not correcting enough, because you can't tell whether a small change failed or was just too small. Trying extremes generally beats timid increments.

![Line chart of behaviour over attempts against a dashed target: the 'overcorrect, then settle' line overshoots the target then converges onto it and lands; the 'timid increments' line rises slowly and is still short at the end](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/overcorrect.svg)

#### Related concepts
- [More dakka](#more-dakka): overcorrecting is more dakka applied to feedback
- [Run the experiment](#run-the-experiment): the overshoot is the experiment - it's how you find where the line actually is

### Smarter first, then harder

When output isn't sufficient: first, work smarter, not harder. Then, work harder.

People get stuck both ways: grinding on the wrong thing, and endlessly optimising instead of putting in the hours.

### Take the risk; run the scream test

When a decision is reversible and the downside is bounded: take the risk.

People don't take enough risk, especially in large organisations. A scream test - turn the thing off and see who screams - is often a surprisingly efficient way to answer "does anyone use this?".

### Use the time-money exchange rate

When weighing effort against spend: convert one into the other - your salary, project deadlines and opportunity costs give you the rate - and use it in both directions.

Spending £100 to save a day is usually great; spending a day to save £100 usually isn't. Most people only ever run the conversion in one direction - happily spending hours to save pounds, while flinching at spending pounds to save hours.

For the time-spent-automating direction, [xkcd has the canonical chart](https://xkcd.com/1205/):

### Find another way

When blocked: generate the second and third route to the outcome instead of stopping at the first "no".

There are usually way more routes than the one that's blocked - a different owner, a workaround, a smaller version, doing it yourself. High agency is mostly this habit, plus a bias to action.

The classic demonstration is [Apollo 13's CO2 scrubber problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_13#Carbon_dioxide_removal): the lunar module's round scrubber cartridges were running out, and the command module's spares were square. The route of having the right part was gone. Ground crew designed an adapter overnight from only what was aboard - plastic bags, cardboard from the flight plan, suit hoses, duct tape - and read the assembly instructions up by radio.

![Engineers crowded around a console at NASA Mission Control, holding up the improvised 'mailbox' adapter prototype of bags, hose and tape built to fit Apollo 13's square CO2 scrubber cartridge to a round hole](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/apollo13-mailbox.jpg)

*(NASA, [S70-35013](https://images.nasa.gov/details/S70-35013): Mission Control demonstrates the improvised adapter. Public domain.)*

#### Related concepts
- [Own it all the way down](#own-it-all-the-way-down): you can only route around a blocker you understand precisely
- [Take the risk; run the scream test](#take-the-risk-run-the-scream-test): the second route often just needs someone willing to try it

### Do the obvious things

When you or someone else needs advice: check whether the obvious thing has been tried.

The obvious advice - sleep, write the list, send the email, ask the question - is usually correct and usually not being done. ([Obvious advice](https://mindingourway.com/obvious-advice/).)

#### Related concepts
- [Run the meeting in your head first](#run-the-meeting-in-your-head-first): "what would I tell someone else in this position?" is the fastest generator of obvious advice
- [Refuse the idiot plot](#refuse-the-idiot-plot): the social version - the obvious question, actually asked

## People

### Climb the user-contact ladder

When deciding how to understand users: climb as high as you can on: be a user > talk to users with good questions > [role-play as a user](../empathetic-role-playing/) > imagine what they want > don't think about them at all.

Each rung down loses information. "Good questions" means [Mom Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mom_Test) questions: ask about their life and past behaviour, not about your idea, because people lie to be nice.

![A ladder with five rungs from best to worst: be a user; talk to users with good questions; role-play as a user; imagine what they want; don't think about them at all. An arrow labelled 'information' points up](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/ladder.svg)

![A staircase descending through the same five options, with a dashed arrow: each rung down loses information](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/ladder-stairs.svg)

#### Related concepts
- [Framestorming](../framestorming/): user contact feeds better problem framings
- [Find problems, not applications for solutions](../find-problems-not-applications-for-solutions/): the problems live up the ladder

### Refuse the idiot plot

When there's tension, ambiguity, or an elephant in the room: say the awkward or obvious thing; ask the dumb question.

Films where everything would resolve if someone just had the obvious conversation are called "[idiot plots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idiot_plot)" - the plot needs the bubble left unpopped. Real life doesn't: you can just pop it, and it resolves things startlingly often. The awkwardness is smaller than predicted, every time.

#### Related concepts
- [Find the crux](#find-the-crux): the awkward question is frequently where the crux was hiding
- [Do the obvious things](#do-the-obvious-things): this is the interpersonal special case

### Disagree and commit

When a decision has been made against your view, on reasonable grounds under real uncertainty: commit fully - no dragging, no relitigating, no "I told you so" positioning.

*Boundary:* genuinely new information reopens the question; your old arguments do not.

The phrase was popularised by [Bezos's 2016 shareholder letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders). He thought a particular Amazon Studios show wasn't worth greenlighting - debatable appeal, complicated to produce, bad business terms - but the team disagreed:

> I wrote back right away with "I disagree and commit and hope it becomes the most watched thing we've ever made." Consider how much slower this decision cycle would have been if the team had actually had to convince me rather than simply get my commitment.

Note the two-sidedness: the team got genuine backing, not grudging backing - and no "I told you so" banked for later.

#### Related concepts
- [Hold concrete opinions, update honestly](#hold-concrete-opinions-update-honestly): commitment and updating are compatible; relitigating and updating are not
- [Accept redundancy](../accept-redundancy/): under real uncertainty, letting a decision you disagree with run is another shot on target

### Hold concrete opinions, update honestly

When forming or stating views: make them concrete, clear and able-to-be-disagreed-with - and visibly update as evidence arrives.

This is the useful version of "strong opinions, loosely held". Vague opinions can't be tested; strongly-performed opinions that never update are worse. ([A good critique of the original phrase](https://glowforge.com/blog/article/strong-opinions-loosely-held-might-be-the-worst-idea-in-tech).)

[Tetlock's superforecasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superforecaster) embody the good version: they state numeric probabilities (concrete, disagreeable-with) and update them in small, frequent steps as evidence arrives. They beat both confident pundits who never budge and vague hedgers who never said anything testable.

#### Related concepts
- [Adopt a scout mindset](#adopt-a-scout-mindset): the underlying disposition
- [Making decisions with made-up statistics](#making-decisions-with-made-up-statistics): probabilities are the concrete form of an opinion

### Run the meeting in your head first

Before asking your boss (or anyone) for something: imagine you're the one being asked. What questions would you ask? What information would you want? What advice would you give?

Have those answers ready, then the follow-ups too. Everyone already does this for job interviews - rehearsing the likely questions is what preparation means there. The routine is doing it for every consequential ask, not only the ones with "interview" in the calendar invite.

This generalises: keep imagined mentors and ask what they'd tell you; predict how people will react before you act.

#### Related concepts
- [Do the obvious things](#do-the-obvious-things): imagined-you usually gives obvious advice; take it

### Overcommunicate

When something matters: repeat it, across channels and time.

Most people weren't listening the first time and will forget the second. Aviation internalised this: pilots read every clearance back word by word, because "I said it once, clearly" is how planes crash. Assuming one announcement was received isn't respectful of people's attention - it's just wrong about how attention works.

## Compounding

### Sharpen the axe

When a manual task recurs: invest in tools.

The name is from the (probably apocryphal) Lincoln line: "give me six hours to chop down a tree and I will spend the first four sharpening the axe." Tool-building feels like procrastination in the moment, and compounds like almost nothing else afterwards. The usual trap is "this is the last time I'll need to do this" - it never is.

### Build systems, not willpower

When you want a good process to stick: build systems that encourage good processes, don't just ask for good processes.

A checklist in the template beats a norm; a lint rule beats a review comment; an automation beats both.

### Recursively self-improve

Periodically: spend time improving how you improve - retros, post-mortems, feedback loops on the feedback loops.

It works for humans, teams and processes, not just AI. The industrial example is [Toyota's kaizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaizen): decades of small, continuous process improvements - including improvements to the improvement process itself - compounding into a manufacturing lead competitors couldn't copy by buying the same machines, because the machines were never the point.

- [Overcorrect, then settle](#overcorrect-then-settle) and [more dakka](#more-dakka): useful moves inside the improvement loop

### Think in public

When you've figured something out: write it where others can see.

You get critiques (free error-correction), and opportunities come to you rather than the reverse - Henrik Karlsson describes a blog post as [a very long search query to find fascinating people](https://www.henrikkarlsson.xyz/p/search-query). I've written about the opportunity side (with my own examples, like the Keycloak consulting offer) in [work in public](../create-surface-area-for-luck/#work-in-public).
