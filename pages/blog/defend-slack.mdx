When planning capacity: you shouldn't usually operate at maximum capacity. Capacity could be time, money, people, social capital or any other constraint.

Having [slack](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2017/09/30/slack/) is what lets you absorb disruption, [seize opportunities](../create-surface-area-for-luck/#prepare-to-seize-windows-of-opportunity), and maintain executive functioning.

This doesn't mean not [working hard](../sometimes-you-just-have-to-grind/). In fact, you can work extremely hard, and still have slack: you can create slack either by spending less capacity, or figuring out how you can add more capacity. Someone who works 100-hour weeks with the capacity to work 110 has slack, someone who works 10 hours with capacity to work 10 doesn't.

This is not just "be nice to yourself" - it also makes you more productive. [Queueing theory and empirical experiments](https://erikbern.com/2018/03/27/waiting-time-load-factor-and-queueing-theory.html) show that with variable-sized tasks at high utilisation, the time it takes you to react to things shoots way up.[^queueing] This is really bad! Being able to react quickly makes you far more able to [learn from feedback](../build-feedback-loops/) and [iterate with a short cycle time](../shorten-cycle-times-reduce-batch-sizes/).

For example, hospitals deliberately keep ~15% of beds empty, because [above ~85% occupancy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10406748/), emergency admissions start hitting dangerous queues.

![Curve of delay against utilisation: nearly flat until high utilisation, then shooting up towards infinity at 100%. Annotations mark 75-85% as leaving slack to absorb shocks, and ~100% as everything queueing](../../images/blog/thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/slack-curve.svg)

Even ignoring the other direct benefits, adding slack to your plans helps combat [planning fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy), where people expect things to go better than they actually will, and also underestimate the time needed to do tasks.

[^queueing]:
    For the quantitative version, see [queueing theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queueing_theory): with unpredictable arrivals, waiting time grows roughly in proportion to `1/(1-utilisation)`. At 90% busy, jobs wait about nine times as long as they take to do; at 99%, ninety-nine times. (People [argue about the exact right hospital occupancy](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0951484819870936) - it depends on ward size and variance - but nobody argues it's 100%.)
