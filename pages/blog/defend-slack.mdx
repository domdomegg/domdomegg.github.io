When planning capacity: you shouldn't usually operate at maximum capacity. Capacity could be time, money, people, social capital or any other constraint.

Having [slack](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2017/09/30/slack/) is what lets you absorb disruption, [seize opportunities](../create-surface-area-for-luck/#prepare-to-seize-windows-of-opportunity), and maintain executive functioning.

This doesn't mean not [working hard](../thinking-tools-to-be-more-effective-at-anything/#smarter-first-then-harder). In fact, you can work extremely hard, and still have slack: you can create slack either by spending less capacity, or figuring out how you can add more capacity. Someone who works 100-hour weeks with the capacity to work 110 has slack, someone who works 10 hours with capacity to work 10 doesn't.

Queueing theory makes the case quantitatively. In any system where work arrives unpredictably, waiting time doesn't rise smoothly with utilisation - it rises roughly in proportion to `1/(1-utilisation)`. At 50% utilisation, a job waits about as long as it takes to do. At 90%, it waits about nine times as long. At 99%, ninety-nine times. The curve is nearly flat for most of its length and then goes vertical, which is why the last few percent of "efficiency" are so expensive.

This is why sysadmins don't run servers at 100% CPU, why motorways jam long before every lane-metre is occupied, and why hospitals plan to keep beds empty. [The classic simulation of NHS emergency admissions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10406748/) found risks appearing above ~85% bed occupancy and regular bed crises at 90%+, which is where the widely-cited 85% target came from. (Queueing people [argue about the exact number](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0951484819870936) - it depends on ward size and how much variance you'll tolerate - but nobody argues the right answer is 100%.) Your calendar is the same system: a fully-booked week has no room for the urgent thing, so the urgent thing displaces something else, and the delay propagates.

Even ignoring the other direct benefits, adding slack to your plans helps combat [planning fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy), where people expect things to go better than they actually will, and also underestimate the time needed to do tasks.
